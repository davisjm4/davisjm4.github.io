# Classroom teacher guide — VEX Component Challenge

## Purpose and entry point

Open CS Games → Robotics → VEX Component Challenge. Use this activity to practice identifying the mechanical function a problem needs, selecting suitable hardware and explaining the decision. VEX Parts Sprint remains the identification practice; Course Architect remains the scaled planning tool. A student can start Mixed Practice without completing either earlier activity.

Begin with Beginner difficulty. Intermediate and Advanced should add constraints and diagnostic reasoning, not merely harder vocabulary. Read the stated assumptions together: they determine which answer is defensible. Components that look similar may require different interfaces, shaft sizes or mounting patterns.

## A five-minute warm-up

1. Use Mixed Practice or select one mode that matches today's build. Spend about three minutes on careful answers; no speed target is needed.
2. Have students read the explanation after an incorrect choice, identify the misunderstood function and try a correction.
3. Finish with one spoken or notebook sentence: “The problem required ___, so I selected ___ because ___.”

Prefer several thoughtful rounds over rapid guessing. First-try accuracy shows the original independent decisions; successful corrections show subsequent learning. They are different evidence and should remain separate.

## A ten-minute review

Use two minutes to discuss one familiar mechanism, five minutes for focused rounds and three minutes for comparison and reflection. Assign Choose the Component when students confuse functions, Fix the Build after a workshop difficulty, Build a Solution when they need to coordinate multiple component roles, and Tool and Fastener Match before tool selection.

For multi-part solutions, ask students to explain the entire set. A plausible part does not make an incomplete or incompatible combination sufficient. Read feedback for missing and extra selections, then revise the set. If a question permits more than one documented solution, discuss the stated constraints that make each one acceptable.

## Before a physical build

Choose the relevant category and demonstrate one actual component. Ask students to identify its interface and what remains unknown from a picture alone. After practice, have the team find the real hardware, compare dimensions, inspect the mounting locations and explain its intended function before tightening anything. Follow the applicable VEX build instructions and classroom tool procedures.

Useful teacher prompts include: “What must rotate?”, “What must stay fixed?”, “Where could this rub?”, “What observation would distinguish those two possible faults?”, and “Which stated measurement matters?” Avoid treating added motor power as a general solution to mechanical interference.

## Students temporarily not building

Use Component Challenge as an alternate practice station alongside the assigned STEM Lab alternative. Set a short focused session and require a brief explanation of one decision, one corrected misconception and one diagnostic check the student would perform with real hardware. Students may sketch or annotate the mechanism and describe what evidence would confirm the proposed fix.

This activity is a supplement. It does not replace the lab's planning, measurements, experiment, notebook evidence or later demonstration with physical parts. It should not be used to claim physical assembly proficiency for a student who has not assembled or tested the mechanism.

## Reference panel and independent practice

The component reference uses the same hardware records as Parts Sprint. Consult it for a part's function, context and official source. Looking up a reference is learning assistance; it must not be mistaken for an independent first attempt. Do not ask students to infer an exact fastener size, material or fit from an image that lacks the required information.

Students should be able to use the game through ordinary keyboard controls and selection buttons; dragging is not required. Check the actual classroom device and assistive-technology setup before assigning independent use. Layout checks do not establish real touchscreen operation or screen-reader speech quality.

## Suggested reflection questions

Use any two or three rather than a long worksheet:

1. What mechanical function did the problem require, and why did your selection meet it?
2. Which constraint or measurement ruled out another plausible choice?
3. What did an incorrect attempt reveal about your reasoning, and how did you correct it?
4. What would you inspect or measure on the physical robot before accepting this solution?
5. What alternative could work under different constraints, and what would need to change?

## Assess the evidence appropriately

A practice score records responses to supplied scenarios. It is not proof that a student can identify a part in a mixed hardware bin, select the correct size, assemble it safely or diagnose an unfamiliar physical mechanism. Combine practice evidence with observation, the engineering notebook and an explanation using the team's actual build.

Use session reporting to discuss coverage and progress, not to reward guessing. Device-local work does not automatically follow a student to another browser or computer.
