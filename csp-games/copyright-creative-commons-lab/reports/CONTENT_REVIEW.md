- **ncnd**: [CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/legalcode.en)
- **zero**: [CC0 1.0](https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/)
- **tasl**: [Creative Commons — Attribution practices](https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/Recommended_practices_for_attribution)
- **fair**: [U.S. Copyright Office — Fair use factors](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/more-info.html)

The six CC 4.0 combinations are checked separately. ND 4.0 legal code sections 2(a)(1) and 2(a)(4) distinguish private production from sharing adaptations and purely technical format changes. Section 1 treats synchronization of music with moving images as adaptation. SA applies to shared adaptations, not merely adjacent separate works. CC FAQ explains NC depends on the use rather than an organization’s nonprofit status and documents collections and remix compatibility. BY 4.0 attribution includes supplied notices, license/source links, current and prior modification indications; supplied titles remain good practice, while the separately marked 3.0 scenario preserves that version’s title requirement. CC0 and the Public Domain Mark are different tools. No fair-use question purports to compute a final legal result from a threshold or a single factor.

### automatic-protection

Copyright Basics · foundational · Sources: [basics](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/)

A student draws an original game character and saves the drawing. They have not registered it or added a copyright symbol.

**Decision:** What should a classmate assume before copying it into a public game?

**Best answer:** Copyright can apply; check reuse permission.

**Rule:** In the U.S., original creative expression is generally protected when fixed in a tangible form, including a saved digital drawing. Registration and protection are different questions.

- Alternative: Copy it freely until the artist registers it. — Registration is not required for copyright to arise.
- Alternative: Copy it freely because it has no copyright symbol. — A copyright symbol is not a prerequisite for protection.
- Alternative: Publish it after adding your own copyright symbol. — A new notice does not transfer the artist’s rights.

### copy-not-copyright

Copyright Basics · foundational · Sources: [basics](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/)

A student buys a downloadable comic. The purchase grants personal reading access, with no redistribution license. They want to upload its pages for everyone.

**Decision:** Which action is best supported by the purchase?

**Best answer:** Read the copy; seek permission before uploading its pages.

**Rule:** The stated purchase permits reading, not public redistribution. A separate permission or applicable exception would be needed. This question concerns uploading copies, not resale of a particular physical copy.

- Alternative: Upload the pages because buying transfers copyright. — Owning a copy does not itself transfer copyright.
- Alternative: Upload the pages if the author is credited. — Credit does not supply the missing distribution permission.
- Alternative: Upload the pages if downloads are free. — Free redistribution still involves the owner’s rights.

### credit-not-permission

Copyright Basics · foundational · Sources: [basics](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/)

A photographer’s portfolio says all rights reserved. A student wants to place a complete photo on a public club site and add a credit. No reuse permission or exception has been established.

**Decision:** What is the best next action?

**Best answer:** Obtain permission or use a suitably licensed photo.

**Rule:** Credit acknowledges the source but does not authorize this public copying. An applicable exception could change a real case; none is established here.

- Alternative: Post it with credit because credit grants permission. — Attribution does not grant a copyright license.
- Alternative: Post it because public websites invite copying. — Viewing access is different from reuse permission.
- Alternative: Post it with a link instead of the photographer’s name. — A link alone does not authorize making a copy.

### personal-license-scope

Copyright Basics · applied · Sources: [basics](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/)

A music download explicitly permits personal listening only. A student wants to include the entire recording as background in a publicly posted game trailer.

**Decision:** What should the student do before publishing?

**Best answer:** Obtain suitable reuse rights or replace the recording.

**Rule:** Permission for one use does not automatically authorize a different use such as combining and publicly sharing the recording. No definitive fair-use ruling follows from these limited facts.

- Alternative: Publish because personal listening includes public trailers. — The stated license does not cover that new use.
- Alternative: Publish because the trailer is a student project. — Student status does not expand this license.
- Alternative: Publish because the recording was downloaded legally. — Lawful access does not establish public reuse rights.

### idea-versus-expression

Copyright Basics · foundational · Sources: [basics](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/)

A student likes the idea of a maze game. They write their own code and draw all new art without copying another game’s expression. Consider copyright only.

**Decision:** Which action is best supported?

**Best answer:** Use the general idea with independently created expression.

**Rule:** Copyright protects original expression rather than the underlying idea or method. This does not settle separate trademark, patent or contractual questions.

- Alternative: Seek copyright permission for the idea of every maze. — Copyright does not protect an abstract idea itself.
- Alternative: Copy the original artwork because the idea is free. — Unprotected ideas do not make the artwork unprotected.
- Alternative: Copy the original source because the gameplay is familiar. — Source code can contain protected expression.

### permission-limits

Copyright Basics · applied · Sources: [basics](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/)

An artist gives written permission to use an icon in one classroom slideshow. The student now wants to distribute that icon in a public asset pack.

**Decision:** Which action respects the stated permission?

**Best answer:** Request permission covering the public asset pack.

**Rule:** The new distribution falls outside the express permission. Ask for a suitable grant or use another asset. Do not assume a private permission is a public Creative Commons license.

- Alternative: Publish because permission for any use covers all uses. — The permission is limited to a stated use.
- Alternative: Publish because the asset pack credits the artist. — Credit does not expand the permission.
- Alternative: Publish because the asset pack is educational. — An educational label does not expand the agreement.

### uploader-authority

Copyright Basics · nuanced · Sources: [faq](https://creativecommons.org/faq/)

An account reposts a famous illustrator’s recent work and labels it CC BY 4.0. The illustrator’s own site does not offer that license, and the account gives no evidence of authority.

**Decision:** What is the best-supported action?

**Best answer:** Verify authorization before relying on the repost’s license.

**Rule:** Only a rights holder or authorized person can grant the relevant permissions. The repost alone is insufficient evidence. Creative Commons does not certify ownership of every labeled work.

- Alternative: Rely on any CC badge, regardless of who added it. — A label cannot grant rights the uploader lacks.
- Alternative: Assume the illustrator lost copyright when it was reposted. — Unauthorized reposting does not transfer copyright.
- Alternative: Treat the picture as public domain because licenses conflict. — Conflicting claims do not establish public-domain status.

### free-access-not-reuse

Copyright Basics · applied · Sources: [basics](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/)

A website lets anyone read a new illustrated story for free. It gives no copying license. A student plans to reproduce the complete story on another site.

**Decision:** What does the free access establish?

**Best answer:** Reading access, not automatic republication permission.

**Rule:** The price of access and the scope of reuse rights are separate. Check for a suitable permission or applicable exception before republication.

- Alternative: A right to republish the complete story with credit. — Credit does not create the missing permission.
- Alternative: That the story has entered the public domain. — A zero price does not end copyright.
- Alternative: A right to replace the author’s name when reposting. — Free access does not transfer authorship or rights.

### license-not-transfer

Copyright Basics · applied · Sources: [by](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/), [basics](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/)

A creator releases an original texture under CC BY 4.0. A student follows its terms and uses it in a game.

**Decision:** What should the student say about ownership?

**Best answer:** The creator retains copyright and grants reuse rights.

**Rule:** A public license grants specified permissions without transferring the underlying copyright. The student can own their original contributions without owning the borrowed texture.

- Alternative: The student now owns copyright in the original texture. — Using a licensed work does not transfer ownership of it.
- Alternative: The creator abandoned copyright by using CC BY. — CC BY is a license, not a public-domain dedication.
- Alternative: Creative Commons now owns the original texture. — Creative Commons supplies tools; it does not take ownership.

### permission-and-exception

Copyright Basics · nuanced · Sources: [faq](https://creativecommons.org/faq/), [fair](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/more-info.html)

A student’s proposed use is outside a photo’s license. A classmate says the license automatically eliminates fair use.

**Decision:** Which response best distinguishes the two?

**Best answer:** Assess any applicable exception separately from license permission.

**Rule:** A license and a legal exception are different possible grounds for use. Fair use requires a fact-specific analysis. This distinction does not declare the student’s particular use lawful.

- Alternative: Every use outside a license is automatically fair use. — Lack of license permission does not establish an exception.
- Alternative: A restrictive license always cancels legal exceptions. — CC licenses do not remove applicable copyright exceptions.
- Alternative: Credit converts an out-of-license use into fair use. — Credit alone does not determine fair use.

### verified-public-domain

Public Domain · foundational · Sources: [basics](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/), [faq](https://creativecommons.org/faq/)

A reliable archive confirms that an original illustration is in the U.S. public domain. There are no separate protected additions or other rights at issue in this case.

**Decision:** What may a student generally do under copyright?

**Best answer:** Copy, adapt and share without copyright permission.

**Rule:** Public-domain material can generally be reused without copyright permission. Citing provenance remains good academic practice.

- Alternative: Ask the former copyright owner for every adaptation. — The stated public-domain work has no copyright restriction on that reuse.
- Alternative: Use it only if the student does not make money. — Public-domain copyright status is not limited to noncommercial uses.
- Alternative: Treat it as CC BY and require a license payment. — Public domain is not the CC BY license.

### free-is-not-domain

Public Domain · foundational · Sources: [basics](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/)

A creator offers a new wallpaper as a free download for personal use. A student wants to sell prints of it.

**Decision:** Which conclusion is supported?

**Best answer:** Free downloading does not establish public-domain reuse rights.

**Rule:** Free access, public-domain status and a reuse license are different. Look for rights covering the intended use rather than inferring them from price.

- Alternative: A zero price makes selling copies unrestricted. — Price does not establish copyright status.
- Alternative: A download button automatically grants CC0 rights. — CC0 must not be inferred from a download button.
- Alternative: Personal-use permission automatically includes resale. — The stated permission is narrower than resale.

### old-but-unknown

Public Domain · foundational · Sources: [basics](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/)

A scanned photograph looks old, but its date, creator and publication history are unknown. A student wants to publish it as a public-domain asset.

**Decision:** What is the best next action?

**Best answer:** Research its status; appearance alone is insufficient.

**Rule:** Copyright duration depends on facts and legal rules, not merely an old appearance. The game does not use a universal age cutoff.

- Alternative: Declare it public domain because it looks old. — Age by appearance does not establish the relevant facts.
- Alternative: Declare it public domain because it was scanned. — Digitization does not automatically establish public-domain status.
- Alternative: Declare it CC BY because its creator is unknown. — Unknown authorship does not supply a license.

### cc-versus-domain

Public Domain · foundational · Sources: [by](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/), [zero](https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/)

One icon is validly offered under CC BY 4.0; another is validly dedicated with CC0 by its rights holder. No other rights are involved.

**Decision:** Which distinction should guide reuse?

**Best answer:** CC BY has attribution conditions; CC0 has no attribution condition.

**Rule:** CC BY licenses rights with conditions. CC0 attempts to waive copyright and related rights to the extent legally possible. Credit can still be good practice for CC0 even when not a copyright condition.

- Alternative: Both require the same CC BY attribution conditions. — CC0 is a different legal tool from CC BY.
- Alternative: Neither can be used in a commercial project. — Neither tool imposes a noncommercial restriction.
- Alternative: Both become owned by the first student to download them. — Downloading does not transfer ownership of the original.

### cc0-scope

Public Domain · applied · Sources: [zero](https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/)

An artist validly applies CC0 to their own original sound effect. The file contains no third-party material or other rights issues. A student wants to edit and sell it in a game.

**Decision:** What does CC0 generally allow here?

**Best answer:** Modify and reuse commercially without copyright permission.

**Rule:** CC0 is a public-domain dedication tool with a fallback license, operating to the extent legally possible. It is separate from the six standard CC licenses.

- Alternative: Only unchanged sharing with CC BY credit. — CC0 does not impose BY or ND conditions.
- Alternative: Only school use with separate commercial permission. — CC0 does not impose an NC restriction.
- Alternative: No reuse until Creative Commons approves the game. — Creative Commons does not approve individual uses.

### other-persons-rights

Public Domain · nuanced · Sources: [zero](https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/)

A CC0 photograph clearly shows an identifiable student. A company wants to use it in an advertisement implying that student endorses a product.

**Decision:** What should the company check?

**Best answer:** Privacy, publicity and endorsement issues beyond copyright.

**Rule:** A public-domain dedication does not clear every right related to a subject or a proposed use. Copyright permission and endorsement permission are separate.

- Alternative: Nothing, because CC0 clears every possible right. — CC0 does not remove other people’s privacy or publicity rights.
- Alternative: Only whether the photographer received a credit. — A credit does not resolve the person’s rights.
- Alternative: Only whether the advertisement uses fewer than ten words. — A word count does not settle these separate rights.

### protected-new-additions

Public Domain · applied · Sources: [basics](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/)

A public-domain novel is published in a new edition with original modern illustrations. A student wants to copy the novel and those illustrations into an app.

**Decision:** Which action is best supported?

**Best answer:** Reuse the novel; check rights for the new illustrations.

**Rule:** Public-domain source material and newly protected contributions can coexist in one edition. Identify which elements the status statement actually covers.

- Alternative: Copy everything because the novel is public domain. — New original additions can have separate protection.
- Alternative: Assume the new illustrations restore copyright to the novel. — New additions do not recreate copyright in the public-domain text.
- Alternative: Use the illustrations without permission if the publisher is credited. — Credit does not clear the separate illustration rights.

### domain-mark-not-waiver

Public Domain · applied · Sources: [faq](https://creativecommons.org/faq/), [zero](https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/)

An archive uses the Public Domain Mark for a work it identifies as free of known copyright restrictions. A student confuses this with the creator applying CC0.

**Decision:** Which explanation best distinguishes them?

**Best answer:** The mark describes status; CC0 attempts a rights-holder dedication.

**Rule:** Public-domain identification and a tool used to waive rights serve different functions. Verify provenance and scope rather than treating any symbol as a universal clearance.

- Alternative: The mark grants the same conditional license as CC BY. — The Public Domain Mark is not a CC BY license.
- Alternative: CC0 requires every reuser to license adaptations as CC0. — CC0 does not impose ShareAlike.
- Alternative: The mark guarantees that privacy and trademark rights are cleared. — A status mark is not a guarantee about all other rights.

### jurisdiction-status

Public Domain · nuanced · Sources: [faq](https://creativecommons.org/faq/), [basics](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/)

An archive says a poem is public domain in one country. A student plans a U.S. publication, but no U.S. status information is given.

**Decision:** What is the best-supported next step?

**Best answer:** Check U.S. status before publication.

**Rule:** A status statement for one jurisdiction need not answer the status question in another. This lab defaults to U.S. principles and does not calculate international terms.

- Alternative: Assume public-domain status is identical everywhere. — Copyright rules and terms can vary by jurisdiction.
- Alternative: Assume it must be CC0 because one country permits reuse. — Public-domain status does not imply a CC0 dedication.
- Alternative: Assume the poem can never be reused in the U.S. — The available information does not establish that prohibition either.

### excluded-third-party

Public Domain · applied · Sources: [faq](https://creativecommons.org/faq/), [zero](https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/)

A collection’s CC0 notice explicitly excludes a cover photograph owned by a third party. A student wants that cover for a public website.

**Decision:** What should the student do?

**Best answer:** Check the cover’s rights separately before copying it.

**Rule:** A dedication or license covers only the material and rights within its scope. Check explicit exclusions instead of extending a collection label to every element.

- Alternative: Use the cover under CC0 because it is in the same download. — The notice explicitly excludes this item.
- Alternative: Use it if the collection’s curator is credited. — Curator credit does not grant the cover owner’s rights.
- Alternative: Assume every collection item is prohibited because one is excluded. — An exclusion does not cancel the stated rights for other items.

### by-commercial

Creative Commons Licenses · foundational · Sources: [by](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)

An original icon is offered by its creator under CC BY 4.0. A student wants to use it unchanged in a paid app; no other rights are involved.

**Decision:** Which action follows this license?

**Best answer:** Use it commercially while meeting the attribution conditions.

**Rule:** CC BY permits commercial sharing and adaptation when its conditions are followed. A permission can be broad while still imposing obligations.

- Alternative: Use it only if the app is free. — CC BY does not impose an NC restriction.
- Alternative: Use it commercially only after removing the license notice. — Required notices must not be removed.
- Alternative: Use it without credit because the app charges money. — Commercial reuse does not remove attribution conditions.

### sa-shared-adaptation

Creative Commons Licenses · applied · Sources: [sa](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/)

A student edits a CC BY-SA 4.0 illustration into an adaptation and plans to share it publicly. They will provide appropriate credit and mark the edits.

**Decision:** Which licensing action best follows ShareAlike?

**Best answer:** Share the adaptation under CC BY-SA 4.0.

**Rule:** Shared adaptations must use the same license or a specifically compatible license as applicable. Using the same version is a valid choice here. SA does not mean every unrelated work by the student needs that license.

- Alternative: Share the adaptation as all rights reserved. — That does not meet the adaptation’s ShareAlike requirement.
- Alternative: Share the adaptation under CC BY-ND 4.0. — Adding ND is not the same or a compatible adaptation license.
- Alternative: Share the adaptation under CC BY-NC 4.0. — Adding NC and dropping SA does not satisfy this license.

### nd-unchanged-sale

Creative Commons Licenses · foundational · Sources: [nd](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nd/4.0/legalcode.en)

A creator offers an original poster under CC BY-ND 4.0. A student wants to sell unchanged copies with all required attribution and notices. No other rights are involved.

**Decision:** What does this license permit?

**Best answer:** Selling unchanged copies while following its conditions.

**Rule:** CC BY-ND allows commercial redistribution of unadapted material with attribution. NoDerivatives restricts sharing adaptations; it is not a blanket ban on copying.

- Alternative: Only private viewing, with no redistribution at all. — ND still permits sharing unadapted material.
- Alternative: Only free copies because ND means noncommercial. — ND and NC are different restrictions.
- Alternative: Selling edited versions under the same ND license. — The license does not permit sharing adapted material.

### nc-direct-advertising

Creative Commons Licenses · foundational · Sources: [nc](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/)

A business wants to use a CC BY-NC 4.0 photo in an advertisement primarily intended to increase its sales. It will credit the photographer. No exception is being claimed.

**Decision:** Which action is best supported?

**Best answer:** Obtain separate permission for this commercial use.

**Rule:** This expressly sales-directed use does not satisfy the noncommercial restriction. NC concerns the use; a user’s label alone is not decisive.

- Alternative: Use it because credit overrides NC. — Attribution does not remove the NC restriction.
- Alternative: Use it because advertisements are publicly viewable. — Public access does not make a use noncommercial.
- Alternative: Use it because the photo itself is free to download. — The download price does not establish permitted commercial use.

### ncsa-two-obligations

Creative Commons Licenses · applied · Sources: [ncsa](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-sa/4.0/)

A student adapts a CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 diagram for a freely shared, noncommercial study guide with no sales or advertising purpose. They will credit the creator and identify edits.

**Decision:** Which plan follows both named restrictions?

**Best answer:** Share noncommercially under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.

**Rule:** NC limits the use; SA governs licensing of a shared adaptation. Both conditions apply. One satisfied condition does not cancel the others.

- Alternative: Sell the adaptation under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. — Keeping SA does not waive the NC condition.
- Alternative: Share noncommercially as all rights reserved. — Following NC does not waive SA for a shared adaptation.
- Alternative: Share commercially under CC BY 4.0. — This drops both restrictions relevant to the adaptation.

### ncnd-combined

Creative Commons Licenses · applied · Sources: [ncnd](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/legalcode.en)

A student wants to share an unchanged CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 poem on a personal, noncommercial reading page with no commercial purpose. Required credit and notices will be included.

**Decision:** Which plan is allowed under the stated license?

**Best answer:** Share the unchanged poem noncommercially with attribution.

**Rule:** The combination permits noncommercial sharing of unadapted material with attribution. NC, ND and BY each do a different job.

- Alternative: Share a translated version on the same public page. — Translation is an adaptation, which this license does not authorize sharing.
- Alternative: Sell the unchanged poem as a paid download. — Unchanged sharing must still satisfy NC.
- Alternative: Share it without credit because both NC and ND are met. — BY remains a condition as well.

### creator-chooses-license

Creative Commons Licenses · foundational · Sources: [faq](https://creativecommons.org/faq/), [by](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/), [sa](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/), [nd](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nd/4.0/legalcode.en), [nc](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/)

A student owns all rights in an original game sprite. They want others to be able to adapt it commercially, with credit, without requiring ShareAlike.

**Decision:** Which standard license best fits that stated goal?

**Best answer:** CC BY 4.0.

**Rule:** CC BY provides the requested reuse permissions with attribution, without NC, ND or SA. Only someone with the relevant rights can grant them.

- Alternative: CC BY-NC 4.0. — NC would restrict the commercial uses the creator wants to permit.
- Alternative: CC BY-ND 4.0. — ND would restrict sharing adaptations the creator wants to permit.
- Alternative: CC BY-SA 4.0. — SA adds an adaptation licensing obligation the creator does not want.

### nc-organization-not-test

Creative Commons Licenses · nuanced · Sources: [faq](https://creativecommons.org/faq/), [nc](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/)

A nonprofit school wants a CC BY-NC 4.0 photo for a project involving a sponsor. The facts do not explain whether the use is primarily directed toward commercial advantage or monetary compensation.

**Decision:** What is the best-supported conclusion?

**Best answer:** More information about the use is needed to assess NC.

**Rule:** NC focuses on the purpose and character of the use, not simply whether the user is a nonprofit or a school. Clarify the actual use or seek separate permission rather than guessing.

- Alternative: It qualifies automatically because the user is a school. — Organizational status alone does not determine NC.
- Alternative: It fails automatically because a sponsor is mentioned. — The sponsor label alone does not establish the purpose of this use.
- Alternative: It qualifies automatically if the photographer is credited. — Credit addresses BY, not the commercial character of the use.

### irrevocable-compliance

Creative Commons Licenses · applied · Sources: [faq](https://creativecommons.org/faq/), [by](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)

A student obtained a texture under a valid CC BY 4.0 offer and continues to comply with every condition. Later the creator removes the CC badge and asks all compliant users to stop.

**Decision:** What is supported by the earlier license grant?

**Best answer:** The compliant student may continue relying on that grant.

**Rule:** CC licenses are irrevocable for recipients who follow their terms. Preserve evidence of the valid grant. This case explicitly assumes a valid authorized offer and continuing compliance.

- Alternative: Every existing license ends when the badge disappears. — A valid CC grant is not revoked merely by changing the website.
- Alternative: The student must now remove all attribution. — Continuing reuse still requires compliance.
- Alternative: The student now owns copyright because the badge disappeared. — A change in labeling does not transfer copyright.

### no-added-restrictions

Creative Commons Licenses · nuanced · Sources: [by](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)

A team redistributes a CC BY 4.0 image unchanged with attribution. Its download agreement would forbid recipients from making any further copies of that image.

**Decision:** Which action follows the CC license?

**Best answer:** Remove the restriction on uses the image’s CC license permits.

**Rule:** Reusers may not impose additional legal terms or effective technological measures that restrict the licensed permissions. This addresses restrictions on the CC material, not ownership of unrelated original code.

- Alternative: Keep the ban because attribution satisfies every condition. — BY also prohibits added legal restrictions on licensed freedoms.
- Alternative: Keep the ban because the team hosts the download. — Hosting does not grant power to withdraw the license permissions.
- Alternative: Replace the creator’s notice with a new all-rights-reserved notice. — A new notice does not remove the original CC grant.

### tasl-practical

Attribution · foundational · Sources: [tasl](https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/Recommended_practices_for_attribution), [by](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)

A source supplies the title “River Light,” creator Kai, a source link and CC BY 4.0. A student shares the image unchanged on a website.

**Decision:** Which credit is the best practical choice?

**Best answer:** “River Light” — Kai; source link; CC BY 4.0 license link.

**Rule:** Title, Author, Source and License (TASL) is a useful attribution practice; include the actual license link and supplied required information. TASL is guidance, not an inflexible legal formula; version and medium matter.

- Alternative: “River Light” — image found somewhere online. — This omits the supplied creator, source and license information.
- Alternative: Kai; a link to a search engine; no license listed. — A search page is not the supplied source, and the license is missing.
- Alternative: CC BY 4.0; photographer and source intentionally omitted. — The supplied creator and source should be retained.

### source-not-search

Attribution · foundational · Sources: [tasl](https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/Recommended_practices_for_attribution), [by](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)

A student’s CC BY 4.0 credit includes the supplied creator and license link but points only to a search-results page. The original source URL is available.

**Decision:** What should the student improve?

**Best answer:** Use the original source link rather than the search-results link.

**Rule:** Link to the licensed material when reasonably practicable using the supplied source information. A discovery tool is not necessarily the source or author.

- Alternative: Remove the creator because the search engine found the work. — Finding material does not make the search engine its creator.
- Alternative: Replace the license link with the search engine’s logo. — The license should remain identifiable and accessible.

### unknown-creator

## Limits and classroom review

Default U.S. principles and CC 4.0, with one expressly labeled 3.0 attribution comparison. No term calculator, real work reproduction, copyright dispute adjudication, jurisdiction survey, automatic fair-use score or AI rights expansion. The private ND, collection and adaptation cases state the decisive classification instead of asking students to resolve borderline derivative-work law. Educator review should check reading load, distractor plausibility, transfer to actual attribution and whether students understand uncertainty. Technical tests alone do not verify those outcomes.
