Learn / Help — access is more than ownership

Start with the person’s computing goal and the resources already available. Identify the actual constraint rather than inferring ability, interest or access from age, income, disability, language or location. Barriers can overlap; each question specifies which supported distinction or goal to evaluate.

Worked examples

Availability versus affordability: A provider can reach a home and the family has a suitable device. If the required bill exceeds the available budget, a second laptop does not address the service cost. A verified sustainable service option addresses that stated barrier more directly.

Ownership versus suitability: A connected phone can be useful for messages but may not run a required desktop assessment. A reachable compatible workstation can meet that specific task without resolving every future access need.

Counts versus rates: In a fictional example, 40 of 50 people have task access in A (80%), compared with 120 of 200 in B (60%). B has more people with access; A has the higher proportion. Neither result identifies why someone lacks access.

Access affects opportunities to learn, apply for jobs, work, obtain healthcare, communicate and participate in civic services. An intervention can help some people while leaving costs, travel, maintenance, skills, language or interaction barriers unresolved. Providing an opportunity is different from measuring successful use or fair outcomes.

All datasets in this lab are fictional instructional examples, clearly labeled at the display. No generated percentage describes a real population. Charts use the same neutral styling for all bars and have equivalent tables and text.
